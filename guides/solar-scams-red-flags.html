Solar scams and red flags: how to protect yourself

By the Be.Solar editorial team. Updated October 21, 2026.

The sales tactics, contract traps and fake 'government programs' to watch for, and what to do if things go wrong.

Most solar companies are honest, but the industry's fast growth and large ticket sizes attract aggressive sellers and outright fraud. Consumer agencies in several countries have warned about misleading solar sales. These warning signs cover most of the problems.

Red flags in the pitch

  • "Free solar" or "a new government program." There is no program that installs panels for free. Usually it's a lease, PPA or loan.
  • Same-day pressure. Discounts that vanish if you don't sign now.
  • Your bill will be "zero". Most connected homes still pay fixed grid charges.
  • Outdated incentives. A US seller promising a 30% tax credit on an owned system installed after 2025 is wrong.
  • Asking for utility login details or signing documents on a tablet without giving you time to read.
  • Impersonating your utility or claiming to be partnered with it.

Red flags in the contract

  • Large deposits before any work, beyond a modest booking fee.
  • Payment escalators above typical utility increases.
  • No production estimate or performance guarantee.
  • Vague equipment ("tier-1 panels") instead of model numbers.
  • Loan terms with a price much higher than the cash price, signalling hidden fees.
  • Liens or fixture filings on your property not explained to you.

Five checks before signing

  1. Verify the contractor licence with your local licensing board.
  2. Check certification: NABCEP, MCS, SAA or MNRE empanelment as relevant.
  3. Read independent reviews from more than one site, sorted by most recent.
  4. Ask for three local references with systems at least two years old.
  5. Confirm your cooling-off period. Many jurisdictions give a few days to cancel door-to-door sales.

If something goes wrong

  • Raise the issue in writing with the company and keep copies.
  • Escalate to the panel or inverter manufacturer for product faults; warranties often survive the installer.
  • Complain to the relevant regulator: state attorney general or consumer protection office in the US, Trading Standards or the relevant consumer code body in the UK, your state fair trading office in Australia, or consumer forums in India.
  • If your installer has closed, look for a third-party warranty provider or another installer who services orphaned systems.

Frequently asked

Are door-to-door solar sellers always bad?

No, but the format favours pressure. Treat any doorstep offer as one quote among three and never sign on the spot.

How do I know if an incentive is real?

Check official government or utility sites directly rather than links provided by a seller. Our incentives pages link to official sources.
