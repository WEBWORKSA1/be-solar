The US solar tax credit in 2026: what ended and what's left

By the Be.Solar editorial team. Updated October 21, 2026.

The 30% homeowner credit ended after 2025. Here's how leases, PPAs, state programs and batteries fit the new picture.

For more than a decade, the federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) let US homeowners subtract 30% of a solar system's cost from their federal income tax. The 2025 budget law ended it early. Here is what that means for anyone considering solar now.

What ended

According to the IRS, the residential credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. The test is when the system is installed and operating, not when you signed or paid. A homeowner who buys a system in 2026, with cash or a loan, cannot claim the federal credit for it.

If you installed a qualifying system in 2025 or earlier, you claim it on that year's return with IRS Form 5695, and unused credit can carry forward to later years.

What remains: leases and PPAs

Systems owned by a company, where you lease the equipment or buy its power under a power purchase agreement (PPA), can still qualify for a commercial clean-electricity credit (Section 48E) claimed by that company. Industry analyses describe deadlines: projects generally must begin construction by July 4, 2026, or otherwise be placed in service by the end of 2027, with energy storage treated more favourably. New restrictions on components from certain foreign entities also apply.

The provider, not you, receives that credit. Whether you benefit depends on how much of it is passed through as a lower lease payment or PPA rate. Compare offers on the total you will pay over the term.

Incentives that still exist

  • State tax credits and rebates. Several states run their own programs. They are not affected by the federal change.
  • Utility rebates and performance payments. Some utilities pay per watt installed or per kWh produced.
  • Solar renewable energy certificates (SRECs). In states with SREC markets, you earn certificates for your production and can sell them.
  • Property and sales tax exemptions. Many states exempt solar's added home value from property tax, and some exempt equipment from sales tax.
  • Net metering or export credits. The value of exported power is set by your state and utility.

The independent DSIRE database (run by North Carolina State University) lists programs by state.

How the math changed

Losing a 30% credit raises net cost by roughly 43% for the same gross price (paying 100 instead of 70). On a $25,000 system that's $7,500 more out of pocket, typically adding three to five years to payback. Lower installer prices and rising electricity rates partly offset it in high-rate states.

Re-run your numbers with the payback calculator: set the tax credit to 0% for an owned system, then compare against a lease or PPA offer's total cost.

Batteries

Home batteries installed by homeowners lost the federal residential credit at the same time. Batteries in third-party-owned arrangements may still be covered by commercial credits. Several states and utilities offer battery-specific incentives or pay owners to share stored power during grid peaks.

Frequently asked

Can I still claim the credit if I signed a contract in 2025 but my system was installed in 2026?

Under the IRS guidance, the credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, so a 2026 installation generally does not qualify. Confirm the specifics with a tax adviser.

Is leasing better now?

Sometimes. A lease or PPA may carry savings from the commercial credit, but you won't own the system, and escalators and transfer terms matter. Compare 20–25-year totals against buying with cash or a low-rate loan.
